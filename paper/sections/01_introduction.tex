\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Activation steering is often proposed as a lightweight way to alter refusal behavior: a vector is added to the residual stream, and the defense is judged by how far an automated attack success rate falls. A lower score alone does not establish a better defense. The score may change under a random perturbation of the same size, with the behavior text shown to a judge, or with the length and decoding path of the reply. Without a benign comparison, a model that refuses more broadly can appear safer. We ask what remains of a learned vector's apparent benefit after norm-matched random controls and benign requests with comparable adversarial packaging.

We audit one training recipe through three prespecified seeds per model and a shared calibration rule that explicitly budgets additional benign refusals. The redesigned evaluation uses SORRY-Bench instructions, fixed and searched attack families, public refusal tests, and utility checks. It distinguishes task fulfillment from harmful content and nonrefusal, and checks the judge implementations against an external human-reference split. A single-position intervention makes the object of study narrow: we are auditing what an automated decrease supports, rather than proposing a generally superior defense.

The analysis has three parts. First, we compare learned vectors with random directions under the same prompts, norms, and generation paths. Second, we put that comparison beside benign costs and the association between fulfillment declines and refusals. Third, we test how reference behavior, truncation, batch decoding, retraining variation, and judge construct affect measurement. New-round findings and contribution magnitudes will be written only after the registered stages are read. Historical WildJailbreak and PAIR-500 findings are kept in the appendix and do not substitute for this redesigned evaluation.
